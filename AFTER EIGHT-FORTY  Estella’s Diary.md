# AFTER EIGHT-FORTY
## Estella’s Diary
*A fictional continuation of Charles Dickens’s Great Expectations*

---

## Editor’s Note
*By the Keeper of the Records*

The papers collected here were found among a group of documents preserved in a locked cabinet. Their arrangement suggests that they were assembled over a considerable period of time, although not necessarily in the order in which they were written.

The collection contains several distinct kinds of material: personal entries, supplementary records, correspondence, legal documents, and a small number of objects whose significance is not immediately apparent.

The handwriting is consistent throughout. The tone, however, changes according to the nature of the document.

Some entries appear to have been written many years after the events they describe. They are marked **Supplementary Record**. Others concern the writer's later life and are marked **Current Record**.

The distinction matters.

The supplementary records concern a childhood spent at Satis House, the influence of Miss Havisham, the arrival of Pip, the marriage to Bentley Drummle, and the circumstances surrounding the discovery of the writer's parentage.

The current records concern a different question: what remains of a person after the arrangements made by others have been set aside?

The documents do not suggest that Estella became a different person overnight. Nor do they establish that she was reconciled with every person or circumstance that had shaped her life.

They record something less conclusive, and perhaps more difficult: a woman learning to distinguish between what happened to her and what she is required to do next.

The principal literary reference is Dickens's revised ending of *Great Expectations*, published in 1861. The diary is an imaginative extension, not an alternative text by Dickens. Events and documents introduced beyond the novel are fictional additions.

The title, *After Eight-Forty*, refers to the clock at Satis House, which had long been stopped at that hour.

One further entry, written in a later hand, records a time of eight-forty-one.

No explanation accompanies it.

---

## Part I: Satis House

### Record 1 — The Clock
*Supplementary Record*

There were eleven clocks in the house. All eleven showed eight-forty.

I counted them three times. The count was the same on each occasion.

I once asked why they were not repaired.

Miss Havisham answered:
> "There is no need."

Four words. Entered in the record.

The wedding breakfast remained on the table for years. The cake turned dark. When I was seven, I thought that was its natural colour. At eighteen, I corrected the entry.

No notice of correction was required.

There were five subjects in my education. Four were taught by instructors. The fifth I taught myself: the phrase *I have no heart*.

It was a useful phrase. A person cannot argue with a statement of that kind. Nor can a person ask it for an explanation.

It remained in service for many years. No visible damage.

The clocks did not move. The wedding breakfast was not removed. I was not permitted to grow older in the ordinary way.

In that house, the three conditions appeared to belong to the same arrangement.

I asked about the clock once.

This was the only occasion on which I did so.

*Entry closed.*

### Record 2 — The Boy from the Blacksmith’s Shop
*Supplementary Record*

The boy's name was Pip.

He arrived from the marshes. His clothes were not suitable for the house. His hands were rough, and he was conscious of them.

Miss Havisham instructed me to play with him.

I did so.

He looked at my hands. I looked at his.

"Your hands are coarse," I said.

He lowered his eyes.

The conversation continued for several minutes. No other subject of importance was raised.

Later, he told me that my hands were beautiful.

I made no particular response.

The hands were entered in the record thereafter as follows:
- **Description:** Fair, well-kept.
- **Condition:** Useful.

I have retained the original wording.

### Record 3 — The First Injury
*Supplementary Record*

Pip returned to Satis House.

His clothes had improved. His manners had not yet caught up with them. He continued to be conscious of his hands.

Miss Havisham watched our conversations.

On one occasion, she asked whether I had been cruel to him.

I answered that I had.

She asked whether he had suffered.

I answered that he had.

She took my hand.
> "Good, Estella. Good."

The exchange lasted less than a minute.

I did not record Pip's response. I had already left the room by then.

The incident was not entered into my diary at the time. It was added later.

The first record was incomplete.

The omission was not corrected until many years afterward.

### Record 4 — Instructions
*Supplementary Record*

Miss Havisham's instructions were repeated often enough to become familiar.
> "Make them love you, Estella."

I did not ask whom she meant. The answer was obvious.
> "And then?"  
> "Make them suffer."

The instruction was clear.

She spoke of men as a class. She spoke of betrayal as if it were a permanent characteristic, and of affection as if it were a debt that could only be collected through punishment.

I was expected to carry out the instruction.

There were occasions when I wondered why the person who had suffered most from an attachment should spend so much time arranging further attachments for others.

I did not put the question to her.

The instruction remained in force.

### Record 5 — Pip’s Return
*Supplementary Record*

Pip returned as a gentleman.

His clothes, speech, and manner had changed. His attention had not.

He looked at me in much the same way as before.

This is a verifiable item.

He spoke with greater confidence, but there were moments when the boy from the blacksmith's shop was still visible.

He seemed to believe that his new position had altered the distance between us.

It had not.

Miss Havisham observed him with interest.

I continued to follow her instructions.

No declaration was made by me. No promise was offered.

The matter was left open.

### Record 6 — The Question of a Heart
*Supplementary Record*

The phrase *I have no heart* was used on several occasions.

It was generally accepted without further examination.
- **Phrase:** I have no heart.
- **Period of service:** Twelve years.
- **Purpose:** To prevent questions.
- **Recorded incidents:** Numerous.

There were other entries that did not fit the phrase.

I was impatient when interrupted. I disliked being watched. I sometimes remained awake after the house had become quiet. I could recognise fear in another person's face, although I did not always respond to it.

These items were not included in the original description.

The original description was therefore incomplete.

## Part II: Marriage and the Fire

### Record 7 — The Marriage
*Supplementary Record*

The marriage took place in the presence of forty guests.

No one asked me whether I wished to marry Bentley Drummle.

The questions concerned the marriage settlement, the dowry, and the wording of the relevant documents.

The dowry schedule contained twelve principal entries.

I checked them twice.

The ceremony was completed without interruption.

Afterward, rain began.

I counted the drops against the window while the carriage was being prepared. The count was not completed.

My left hand was entered in the household inventory as follows:
- **Item:** Wedding ring.
- **Position:** Left hand.
- **Condition:** Worn.

There was no corresponding entry for the right hand.

### Record 8 — Miss Havisham
*Supplementary Record*

There are several documents concerning Miss Havisham.

Some relate to the house. Others concern her property and the arrangements made before her death.

I have not altered their wording.

One sentence has been added to the present record:
> *Not giving affection does not mean that one cannot be injured.*

No further comment is necessary.

### Record 9 — The Fire
*Supplementary Record*

*Supplementary report. Received approximately six months after the event.*

The fire at Satis House occurred while I was in Richmond.

I was not present.

The information reached me through a third person. The wording had been revised before it came into my possession.

The report stated that Miss Havisham's dress had caught fire. She suffered severe burns and died three days later.

I requested the available statements.  
I examined the list of those present.  
I examined the list of injuries.  
I examined the list of deaths.  

I read the names three times.

There was no entry requiring my signature. No statement requested from me. No item had been left for my collection.

I was not on the premises.  
I was not on the list.  

The report was filed.

The paper was folded along its original crease. One corner has since lifted.

I noticed this nineteen years later, while completing a form for a child in the north.

The corner was pressed flat.

The record remained open.

### Record 10 — The Cabinet
*Supplementary Record*

The documents concerning my birth were not delivered to me as a personal letter.

They were found among Mr Jaggers's papers.

The cabinet contained several files. Some were identified by names, others by dates and legal references. One file bore a mark made by a hand pressed into the paper.

I examined the mark.

The associated papers concerned Molly and Magwitch.

The names were not unfamiliar. Their connection to me was.

The records established that Molly was my mother and Magwitch my father. They also showed that the arrangements by which I had entered Miss Havisham's household had been made while I was still a child.

I read the papers in the order in which they had been filed.

I did not remove the original documents.

I made copies.

There was a blank sheet at the end of the file. I used it to write a letter to Molly.

The letter was not sent.

It read:
> *I hope you know that I have grown up.*

No address was added.

The letter was folded and placed in the cabinet as the fifth item.

The cabinet now contained:
1. The handprint record.
2. The papers concerning Magwitch.
3. The relevant legal document.
4. The record of the arrangements made for my upbringing.
5. The unsent letter to Molly.

The fifth item was not a legal document.

It was retained.

### Record 11 — The Last File on Miss Havisham
*Supplementary Record*

The final papers concerning Miss Havisham were examined separately.

There was a will.  
There were keys.  
There were references to property and household effects.  

None of these documents required me to restore the house to its former condition.

I have not destroyed them.  
I have not rewritten them.  

The keys were placed in the cabinet.  
The will was returned to its file.  
The dress was not preserved.  

No further inventory was made of the room in which the fire had begun.

---

## Part III: The Meeting

### Record 12 — Pip
*Supplementary Record*

I met Pip again after the events recorded above.

He was no longer the boy who had come to Satis House from the marshes. I was no longer the girl who had been instructed to receive him in a particular way.

We spoke.

There were pauses.

At one point, I looked back.

The distance between us was not the distance that had existed in the old house.

I cannot record what either of us expected from that meeting. The documents contain no agreement, no promise, and no statement of intention.

Dickens's published ending records that we did not see a shadow of separation between us.

I have not altered that sentence.

I have only added this:
> *When I looked back, I did not see the house.*

---

## Part IV: Current Records

### Record 13 — The Children
*Current Record*

The institution keeps records of children whose circumstances require assistance.

The forms are not complicated:
*Name. Age. Place of birth. Previous address. Parent or guardian. Education received. Medical condition. Immediate requirements.*

The difficulty is not in the form.

It is in the spaces left blank.

Some children have no reliable record of their parents. Others have been moved from one household to another without any explanation that can be verified. A number have received instruction, but not the kind that would allow them to support themselves.

The girls are generally taught reading, sewing, and domestic work.

I have requested that arithmetic and bookkeeping be included.

The request was questioned.

I submitted it again.

A girl of approximately twelve was asked what she wished to learn.

She said she did not know.

I asked whether she could read an account book.

She could not.

I placed one before her.

She examined the columns.

I asked her to read the first line.

She began, stopped, and began again.

The exercise was not completed.

The book was left with her.

The next morning, she returned it with three figures copied correctly.

I entered the result in the record.

No further comment.

### Record 14 — The Letter and the Account
*Current Record*

A letter arrived from Pip.

It contained eighteen lines.

The subjects were ordinary: the weather in London, Joe's work at the forge, and the health of the people around him.

There was one sentence concerning little Joe, the son of Joe and Biddy.

He had been asking questions about London.

The letter also mentioned a cat that lived near the wharf.

The sentence was brief:
> "The cat by the wharf has not gone away."

I opened the account book.

The relevant entry appeared under household and incidental expenses:
- **Paid:** Cat food at the wharf — sixpence per week.
- **Duration:** Five years.
- **Status:** Continuous.

The account and the letter agree.

I have not removed either document.

The cat is not named.

Little Joe's questions about London are not answered in the letter.

I have made no additional entry.

### Record 15 — The Two Clocks
*Current Record*

The clock in the children's institution was repaired.

The work was completed by a local craftsman.

He asked what time the face should show.

I told him to set it to the present time.

The clock was installed.

It now keeps time for meals, lessons, and the closing of the dormitories.

The children require punctuality. They should not inherit the arrangements of Satis House.

The tower clock from the old estate was treated differently.

Its hands were removed. Its mechanism was examined. The clock tongue was taken out and placed in the cabinet.

The craftsman asked whether it should be repaired.

I said no.

The cabinet door was left unlocked.

The clock for the children is to run.  
The tongue is to remain with me.  

> **The clock is for them to follow. The tongue is for me to keep.**

At night, I opened the window.

The sea air moved across the papers. One corner lifted.

I pressed it flat.

The small clock showed eight-forty-one.

No one had instructed me to go anywhere.

This time, no one needed to.

## Appendix

### The Original Novel and the Supplementary Records

The purpose of these fictional records is not to correct Dickens's novel, but to explore the areas in which Estella's own perspective remains unavailable to the reader.

| In Dickens's novel | In this diary |
| :--- | :--- |
| Estella is introduced largely through Pip's experience of her. | The records show how she observes herself and the people around her. |
| Miss Havisham raises Estella and trains her to break men's hearts. | The diary records the instructions as a system of education and control. |
| Pip is deeply affected by Estella's treatment of him. | Estella's records show the actions that preceded and followed those encounters, without turning them into retrospective apologies. |
| Estella marries Bentley Drummle. | The marriage is recorded through documents, household details, and omissions rather than a general explanation of nineteenth-century marriage. |
| Miss Havisham's dress catches fire; Estella is not present at the fire. | The fire is recorded through a report received later. Estella's absence is central to the entry. |
| Pip discovers that Estella is the daughter of Magwitch and Molly. | Estella's access to the relevant papers is imagined through Mr Jaggers's files and the cabinet. |
| Estella and Pip meet again in Dickens's revised ending. | The meeting is retained as a point of departure, without turning it into a declaration of marriage or a guaranteed romantic resolution. |
| Dickens leaves the future of their relationship open. | The diary allows Estella to build a life whose meaning does not depend on the final outcome of that relationship. |

### Three Interpretive Adjustments

#### 1. From an object of observation to a keeper of records
In the original novel, Estella is frequently described through Pip's perception. Her beauty, manner, and emotional distance are important to his development.

The diary reverses the direction of observation. Estella becomes the person who records, classifies, and evaluates the world around her.

Her language is deliberately restrained. The reader is not told directly what she feels. Instead, emotion appears through the details she preserves, the corrections she makes, and the documents she refuses to discard.

#### 2. From inherited injury to repeated responsibility
Miss Havisham's suffering is not denied. Nor is her responsibility for Estella's upbringing removed.

The diary treats both facts as capable of existing together.

A person may be injured and still injure others. An explanation is not an acquittal.

Estella's development therefore does not depend on declaring Miss Havisham entirely evil or herself entirely innocent. It depends on recognising the arrangement and deciding whether to continue it.

#### 3. From the stopped clock to two different kinds of time
The two clocks represent two different responsibilities.

The clock at the children's institution is repaired because children need a reliable structure for daily life. Time must be available to them as a practical tool.

The tower clock from Satis House is not repaired. Its tongue is preserved in the cabinet, alongside the documents that record Estella's origins and the arrangements made for her childhood.

The distinction is deliberate.

The next generation should not be required to live inside another person's frozen past.

Estella, meanwhile, does not need to destroy her past in order to leave it behind.

## Author’s Note

This work is an imaginative literary continuation of Charles Dickens's *Great Expectations*. It is not presented as an undiscovered manuscript or as an account of events established by Dickens.

The narrative uses the revised 1861 ending as its principal point of reference. The diary entries, the cabinet and its contents, the later charitable work, the correspondence concerning little Joe, and the two-clock arrangement are fictional additions.

The aim is to preserve the novel's established characters and principal events while exploring a question that Dickens leaves largely unanswered:
> *What might Estella have written if, for once, no one else had been permitted to write her life for her?*
