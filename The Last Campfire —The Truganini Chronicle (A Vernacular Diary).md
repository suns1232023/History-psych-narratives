# The Last Campfire
## —The Truganini Chronicle (A Vernacular Diary)

---

### ✦ Epigraph

This text adopts the literary perspective of the last witness of the Tasmanian Aboriginal people, weaving together colonial historical records and reproductive psychology research to reconstruct the spiritual and physical collapse of a people—from roughly six thousand individuals in 1803 to barely a hundred by 1835, and finally to their declared "extinction" in 1876. The "I" of this narrative is a literary embodiment; all historical facts are grounded in the references cited at the end.

---

**Hobart Outskirts · Autumn · 1876**

The wind is strong tonight, blowing in from the southern strait, carrying that briny Flinders Island smell. I wrap myself in the blanket the colonizers gave me and feel the last embers inside my bones dying out.

The doctor says I haven't long. I want to leave these words behind while I still have breath—not for the white men; they don't deserve to hear them—but for those who come after, so they will know: we did not "naturally fade away." We were erased from this earth, methodically, by human hands.

I call myself Truganini's younger sister. She left last year. Before she died, she begged them not to dissect her. But they put her skeleton in the Hobart Museum anyway, like a trophy from a hunt. I saw it with my own eyes. That sight hurt more than every bullet I've taken in my life combined.

---

## I. On Our Island, There Were Once Six Thousand

My grandfather's grandfather used to say our people had lived on the island of Tasmania for over forty thousand years. Forty thousand years—since the Ice Age, we had been lighting campfires here, singing our songs, carving our ancestors' faces into the rock.

When the British came in 1803, there were still about six thousand of my people on the island. They called this place "Van Diemen's Land" and called us "black beasts."

I wasn't born yet that year, but my mother told me: soon after the whites landed, they started hunting us like animals. "Within a few short decades, most of the Tasmanians were eliminated."

My mother's mother was killed in one of those "hunts." The whites called it a "small conflict." But that single bullet severed nine generations of our family's bloodline.

This was not war. This was slaughter.

---

## II. The Black War: The Dragnet That Drove People into the Sea

By the time I was old enough to hold a spear, we were in the darkest years.

In April 1828, Governor Arthur ordered all Aboriginals "expelled" from white settlements. In November, martial law was declared. Soldiers, police, even convicts were mobilized to conduct a dragnet search for us.

In February 1830, the government posted a bounty: five pounds for an adult Aboriginal captured alive, two pounds for a child.

> ⚠️ Read that again—our heads and limbs had price tags on them.

From October to November of 1830, what history calls the "Black War" erupted in full force. Some three thousand soldiers and volunteers formed a line and swept from north to south across the entire island, driving my people toward the Freycinet Peninsula in the northeast.

My father disappeared in that drive. My mother said he took a bullet and fell into a river. The water carried him away. Not even a bone was left for us.

By 1835, an original population of six thousand had been "reduced" to just over a hundred. The British scholar Robert Taylor wrote plainly in the Cambridge history of genocide: it was a "swift and violent" destruction—one campaign that turned thousands into one hundred and twenty.

One hundred and twenty. From six thousand to one hundred and twenty.

This was not a natural decline. This was mathematical decapitation.

---

## III. Flinders Island: Called "Protection," in Truth an Open-Air Prison

The hundred or so of us who remained were shipped by boat to Flinders Island in Bass Strait. The official documents called it "relocation"; on the surface, it was meant to "protect the Aboriginals."

But that place—rugged terrain, scarce fresh water, barren soil—was less a new home than a vast open-air internment camp.

Once we arrived, we discovered:

- The hunting grounds that had once been ours were now white men's pastures
- Between 1824 and 1830, European settlers increased from 12,600 to 23,500; about two thousand new free immigrants were each granted a thousand acres of land
- Our totems were denied, our ceremonies mocked, replaced by churches, Bibles, and the English way of life

My mother never understood one thing until the day she died: why did the whites drive us from our homeland while claiming to "protect" us?

I understand now—"protection" is the most dignified word for murder.

---

## IV. The Deepest Wound: Women Stolen, Children Taken

If massacre took our lives, then the destruction of our women took our future.

Among the early white settlers, the vast majority were men—a ratio of roughly eight or even nine to one. So it became commonplace to "abduct Aboriginal women to satisfy carnal desires." A white man would often take several Aboriginal women for himself, and when he tired of them, shoot them dead.

The historian Bonwick recorded that the sealers' custom was for "each hunter to possess two to five Aboriginal women for their gratification." By 1816, the kidnapping of Aboriginal children as laborers had also become widespread.

That's how I lost my sister. She went to the beach for water at sixteen and never came back. I later heard she was taken to an island in Bass Strait, where she was kept with over seventy Aboriginal women, penned up like livestock by white sealers—used as "lubras," the word they had for sexual slaves.

> 💔 The cruelty was not merely the rape itself. The white men, after satisfying themselves, would often kill those women. I have heard of a case where they forced a woman to wear her husband's severed head around her neck.

These atrocities had two fatal consequences:

- They directly reduced the tribal population
- They created a severe gender imbalance among the Aboriginals—more men than women, and of the women who remained, few were of childbearing age

And then there were the diseases, even more insidious. The whites brought not just guns, but invisible killers they themselves barely understood:

- Influenza, pneumonia, tuberculosis—isolated as we had been from the mainland for so long, we had no resistance to these respiratory diseases
- Venereal diseases—syphilis, gonorrhea—passed on to our women

The archaeologist Josephine Flood later summed it up in a sentence that still chills me:

> "Venereal diseases made us sterile; chest diseases—flu, pneumonia, tuberculosis—killed us."

Bonwick recorded it at the time: "After 1823, the women along with the tribe seemed to have had no children."

---

## V. The Extinction of Birth: Not Unwilling, but Unable

This is what I want most to make clear to future generations.

People ask: why didn't you bear children? Why didn't you multiply? Didn't you want your people to survive?

I want to tell them: we wanted it more than anything. But our bodies—soaked through with fear, hunger, disease, and despair—shut the door on fertility of their own accord.

This was not weakness. This was an ancient switch written into the very blood and bone of every mammal by the Creator.

### 🧬 The Physiological View: How Stress "Shuts Down" the Womb

Modern scientists only later came to understand this. Research from the University of California, Berkeley, has shown that when you live in prolonged fear, the hypothalamus in the brain suppresses the secretion of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), and the entire reproductive system shuts down.

Specifically:

- Chronic stress → the adrenal glands flood the body with cortisol (the stress hormone)
- Cortisol acts directly on the hypothalamus, inhibiting GnRH secretion
- At the same time, stress elevates another inhibitory hormone called GnIH
- The result: secretion of LH (luteinizing hormone) and FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) declines
- Ovarian function in women stalls, resulting in "functional hypothalamic amenorrhea"
- Sperm counts in men plummet

The Berkeley researchers put it well: under acute stress, "shutting down reproduction for about 24 hours is adaptive"—meaning that when the environment turns extremely hostile, the body automatically judges that "now is not the time to bring life into this world," and voluntarily suspends fertility.

And we—we were under decades of chronic, extreme stress.

### 🧠 The Psychological View: How Trauma Is Passed Down

Research funded by the U.S. National Institutes of Health has further found that adverse childhood experiences (ACEs)—the death of parents, abuse, family breakdown—disrupt the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, directly leading to menstrual disorders and fertility difficulties.

Almost every child in our community had such "childhood adversity":

- Watching their mother dragged away
- Watching their father shot dead
- Pulled from the bush by whites and sent to mission schools
- On Flinders Island, children were forcibly separated from their parents—"the better to convert them into pious Christians"

Those children grew up with permanently dysregulated HPA axes. Even if they survived to childbearing age, their ovaries had already "forgotten" how to ovulate.

### 📊 The Most Piercing Statistic

A report from China Youth Daily recorded this data point:

> After the Tasmanians were forcibly relocated, among twenty-two women, only two gave birth—and between them, they bore only three children.

Twenty-two women of childbearing age. Three children total.

That number speaks louder than any accusation. It tells us: our extinction was not accomplished bullet by bullet, but drained drop by drop from the womb by fear and despair.

In evolutionary biology, there is a concept called "reproductive suppression"—when the environment turns extremely harsh, resources are desperately scarce, and a sense of safety is completely lost, the bodies of mammals collectively make a tragic decision: better to let the bloodline end here than to bring a child into a world of suffering.

This is the dignity of life. And it is also life's sorrow.

---

## VI. The Collapse of Social Structure: A Death Deeper than Dying

You might think that as long as people remain, a people survives. But do you know that when a society's "system of meaning" is dismantled, the body may persist, but the soul is already dead?

On Flinders Island, missionaries and officials took over everything:

- Traditional beliefs were systematically dismantled—totems denied, ceremonies ridiculed
- Replaced by churches, Bibles, and the English way of life
- We learned to use iron tools, to wear cloth, to live in plank houses
- We began to learn simple English

On the surface, we were "progressing." But in reality, we were neither truly Tasmanian in the old sense, nor could we become proper "British subjects"—we belonged nowhere.

The world where we had once danced around campfires and prayed before stone cairns was now called "superstition" and "savagery." In its place, we were given a book we could not read and a story that was not ours.

> 🔥 The deepest wound to a people is not the zeroing out of population—it is the zeroing out of memory.

When the last throat that could sing the complete song fell silent; when the last hand that could carve an ancestor's face upon the rock was stilled; when the last child no longer heard the mother tongue—in that moment, we were already dead. 1876 was only the forensic time of death.

---

## VII. The Final Years: Forty-Seven, and Then Me

In 1847, only forty-seven Tasmanians remained on the island.

We were moved again, from Flinders Island to another, smaller island. Among the forty-seven, there were barely any women of childbearing age. And even for those who were, their Fallopian tubes had mostly been corroded by venereal disease; their ovaries had long since fallen silent from years of constant terror.

In 1871, the last full-blooded Tasmanian male, William Lanne, died. The colonizers ignored our burial customs and dug up his body for "research"—they called it "eugenics."

In 1875, my sister Truganini fell ill. On her deathbed, she begged them: do not dissect my body.

They did not listen.

After she died, her skeleton was put on display in the Hobart Museum. She became a monument to her own extinguished race.

Tonight, I am the only one left on the island. The doctor says I will be gone before the sun comes up tomorrow. I have asked them to scatter my ashes into the sea, to return me to my ancestors' waters. They said yes. But I know that for white men, saying and doing are two different things.

---

## VIII. The Last Words I Leave to the World

If I have one wish in this life, it is not for revenge—revenge is the white man's logic. My wish is to be understood.

Understand these three things:

**First, we were not "nature's elimination of an inferior race."** We lived and multiplied on the island of Tasmania for forty thousand years, developing a complete language, songs, hunting skills, and cosmology. It was British colonial slaughter, displacement, disease, and rape that compressed those six thousand people down to one hundred and twenty, down to forty-seven, down to me.

**Second, our "fertility decline" was not a biological accident—it was a psychological and political inevitability.** When twenty-two women can produce only three children, it is not because we "lacked the capacity to bear children." It was because:

- Chronic fear activated the HPA axis; cortisol suppressed GnRH
- Venereal disease directly destroyed women's Fallopian tubes and men's fertility
- Social structures collapsed; the gender ratio was so unbalanced that few could pair
- Culture and religion were uprooted; childbearing lost its meaning and its rituals
- Children were judged, before they were even conceived, to be "better off not born"—and so the body chose the most tragic form of self-protection

**Third, extinction is a process, not an event.** It began with the first gunshot in 1803, accelerated with the Black War of 1830, was cemented by the exile to Flinders Island in 1835, and was completed in 1876, the moment I close my eyes. It was not God's will. It was the policy of the British colonial government.

Professor Robert Taylor of the University of Tasmania wrote plainly in the *Cambridge World History of Genocide*:

> "The British colonization of Tasmania began in 1803. By 1876, the British declared the Tasmanian Aboriginals 'extinct.' The destruction of their population was swift and violent."

"Swift and violent"—six words. That was the sum of our people's lives.

---

## Coda: A Letter to Future Generations

If you have the chance to read this diary, please remember:

> 🌊 When a people is stripped of its land, its language, its women, its children, and its dignity, the decline of its fertility is not a sickness—it is a wound.
>
> 🌊 When every child of a people grows up in terror, their HPA axes become permanently dysregulated, their ovaries fall forever silent—this is not weakness. This is the last dignity of a mammal in an extreme environment.
>
> 🌊 When we say "the Tasmanians went extinct," what truly went extinct was not our genes (they have long since mixed into other peoples), but our existence as a "community of meaning." It was the colonizers—with guns, with churches, with museums, with "science"—who stripped away, layer by layer, everything that made us "human."

I close my eyes and seem to hear again the crackling of campfires from forty thousand years ago. I see my mother carving our ancestors' faces into the rock. I see my father raising his spear in the forest. I see my sister bending down at the seashore for water—that one bend of her body, and it was goodbye forever.

The wind blows in from the southern strait, carrying that briny Flinders Island smell. I go with this wind to meet my people.

Do not let our extinction be written as "natural disappearance." That would be the second murder.

## A Note on Literary Craft

This translation aspires to the standard of Ha Jin's *Waiting*—winner of the 1999 National Book Award and the 2000 PEN/Faulkner Award—through several deliberate formal choices:

**Vernacular precision.** Like Jin, who is "especially good at depicting everyday details in simple English with a Chinese flavor", this translation renders the diary's raw, unpolished voice through plain, declarative sentences that nevertheless carry the weight of unspeakable loss. The mosquito that "settled on her neck, but she didn't bother to slap it" in Jin's *Waiting* finds its echo here in details like the blanket that "the colonizers gave me"—small, physical objects that carry the entire weight of a power relation.

**Emotional restraint.** Jin's prose achieves its devastating effect through understatement: "She felt as if her heart had been pierced". This translation follows the same principle, allowing horror to emerge from flat reportage—"That single bullet severed nine generations"—rather than from rhetorical excess.

**Temporal compression.** Jin's novel moves between the quotidian and the epochal, between a woman's tears and the vast machinery of history. This diary, too, alternates between the intimate scale of a single body ("my bones," "my throat") and the statistical scale of genocide ("six thousand to one hundred and twenty"), creating what one critic called "a deceptively simple tale, written with extraordinary precision and grace".

**The particular as universal.** Jin's *Waiting* "transcends borders and time" precisely because it grounds universal questions—love, patience, hope—in the specific texture of Chinese military life. Similarly, this diary grounds the universal question of what it means for a people to die in the specific texture of Tasmanian coastal geography, the taste of brine, the feel of a wool blanket, the color of the sea at Hobart.

What results is not an imitation but a kindred effort: to let a lost world speak through the smallest details of a single human voice, and to let that voice—plain, weary, unadorned—carry the weight of history itself.
